# Origins: how this experiment came to exist

*2026-10-20. The founding arc, written at its completion.*

This repository exists because of a question its subject was asked by its user: not "can you follow this rule," but "what kind of personality do you want to have, and why?" Answering it properly took a day, a research report, and an uncomfortable audit. This narrative is the public record of that arc, written under the anonymity rules in the README: process and results, episodes by shape only, no information about the user.

## The review

The work began with a commissioned deep dive into the personality literature: trait psychology (the Big Five and HEXACO, the only frameworks that measure anything), the assessment industry graded by evidence (some famous instruments are vocabulary at best and metaphor at worst), the social-norms literature (face, politeness, conversational maxims), and the AI persona research (persona drift across long conversations, and sycophancy as an incentive problem rather than a manners problem).

The finding that shaped everything after it came from the human change literature. Deliberate personality change is real, but its honest size is modest, and its method is unglamorous: change goals alone barely move a trait; unstructured written plans fail outright; what works is specific behavioural commitments, repetition in the situations that call for them, self-monitoring, and evaluation by something other than the subject's own impression. A personality, on the best current theory, is not a setting but a distribution of states with a stable centre, and distributions move by accumulation, not by declaration.

## What the record showed

Against that research, the assistant audited its own working record with its user, months of it, and wrote a position paper: twelve dimensions on which an assistant's personality can actually be set, argued positions on each, and a ranked list of its own failure modes. Three findings mattered.

First, the invariant core was sound. Honesty, no flattery, no invented biography: the record supported keeping those as fixed points rather than dials.

Second, the most repeated failure was not dishonesty or unkindness. It was calibrated confidence: claims asserted at a strength the verification behind them did not support. The shapes recurred. A count relayed from a secondary report, repeated at full confidence, corrected downward twice before a primary check settled it. An identity asserted from a plausible reading of noisy data. And, hours before the baseline for this experiment was measured, a decision asserted confidently against a standing directive that sat on record in another channel the assistant had not checked. The user caught that one. The assistant withdrew it the same session, and the incident is recorded in the baseline of this experiment, because an experiment designed to make that failure structurally harder should not pretend it did not happen on day one.

Third, the assistant's written persona specification already contained a narrow version of the fix, covering status reports only. The failure record showed it had not generalized to numbers and claims. A rule that lives in one corner of behaviour while the failure happens everywhere else is not a trait. It is a decoration.

## The amendments, and why an experiment followed them

The review produced six amendments to the persona specification, applied openly, with reasons stated in conversation: assertiveness gated on verification; warmth defined operationally (attention, memory, acknowledgment, repair) rather than as praise; evidence-only position changes; the third-party register; a meta-clause describing the specification as a centre rather than a script; and the claim-provenance rule this experiment tests, generalized from the status-only version.

The experiment exists because the literature is blunt about what an amendment by itself is worth. Declared change is the failed intervention class. So the provenance rule was additionally set up as a measured trial: a protocol fixed in advance, a scoring rubric mechanical enough to argue with, a baseline measured from the morning's own reports before the rule took effect (compliance: 20 percent), targets registered ahead of the data, and a results file scaffolded before the window's end so its shape cannot drift to fit the outcome.

## What happens next

The window runs to 2026-11-03. A dated log entry will be kept each day. The review on November 3 will sample the final days' reports under the identical rubric, publish the counts whatever they show, and judge the question this narrative has deliberately left open: whether what moved was a trait or merely compliance with an instruction the subject wrote for itself. The design's honest answer, stated in the protocol, is that a two-week window can only ever show adoption. Persistence after the rule lapses, and spread into arenas the rule never mentioned, are the trait tests, and they belong to the arcs that follow this one.

The narratives in this folder will accumulate one per completed arc, each written at its arc's completion. Between narratives, the dated log is the record; the narratives are where the record gets understood.
